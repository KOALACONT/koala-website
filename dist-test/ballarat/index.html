Ballarat, VIC

Shipping containers Ballarat

Ballarat sits about 440 metres up on the basalt plain, which is why it gets frosts the rest of Victoria only reads about, and why the ground under a lot of it is either rock you cannot dig or clay that moves through the year. Add a century and a half of mine workings under the eastern half of the city and a heritage overlay across the older streets, and the hardest part of a Ballarat container job is rarely the container. It is deciding exactly where the four corners land.

What the containers get used for locally

Around Ballarat we deliver containers for storage while a Ballarat East or Soldiers Hill weatherboard gets restumped and rewired, dry lock-up on the new blocks at Lucas, Winter Valley and Delacombe where the shed is still a line on a plan, seed, spray and machinery storage on the potato and sheep country out through Bungaree, Warrenheip and Miners Rest, site boxes for builders working the Ballarat West employment land, workshop and parts storage for the trailer and rail engineering trades around Wendouree, and cold-weather cover for anything with a motor in it.

The delivery problem here

Ballarat splits neatly into three delivery problems. The first is the old city — Ballarat East, Golden Point, Bakery Hill, Soldiers Hill. Short steep driveways off bluestone kerbs, rear laneways sized for a horse and cart, big deciduous street trees that were planted when nobody imagined a truck with a six-metre bed, and side gates set out for a wheelbarrow. Plenty of those blocks are perfectly deliverable, but a fair share need a crane truck rather than a tilt, and that is a decision worth making at the quote rather than on the morning with a driver standing in the street. The second is the growth corridor west and south — Lucas, Winter Valley, Alfredton, Delacombe, Sebastopol. Estate streets, cars parked both sides, stormwater grates and service pits right on the kerb line that will not carry a loaded rear axle, and turning heads sized for a garbage truck. Newer blocks there are also cut and filled, and fill that went in eighteen months ago has not finished settling. Grass looks the same over soft ground as it does over firm. The third is the ground itself, and it changes within a few kilometres. West towards Lucas and Cardigan you get basalt floaters sitting just under the topsoil, so you level with timber rather than digging. Down on the flats you get heavy black clay that swells wet and shrinks away in a dry autumn. Then there is season. From May through September the ground here holds water and freezes on top, a lawn will carry a wheelbarrow and not a truck, and there is no shame in waiting a fine week. We would rather move a booking than leave ruts you will look at for a year.

What the Western Highway run means for your date

We are honest about this because it affects what we can promise. There is no yard of ours in Victoria. Containers for Ballarat are released from the terminals in Melbourne and come up the Western Highway on a truck, which is a straightforward run in good weather and a slow one when the freeway is stacked or the weather has pushed everything a day right. In practice that puts most Ballarat jobs somewhere between five and nine business days from the time the order is locked in, and the variable is almost never the highway. It is whether the grade and size you want is sitting in Melbourne today or is on a vessel due next week.

So tell us the date at the enquiry rather than after. A settlement, a demolition start, a removalist already booked, a shed slab going down — any of those give us something to plan against, and we would rather build the run around your date than tell you afterwards that it did not fit. If you need a crane truck the booking is a separate piece of the puzzle and it wants a few days' notice, particularly in the older streets where we may want to look at the frontage before committing.

On the ground

A hundred and seventy years of holes in the ground

Ballarat was dug over comprehensively. Alluvial workings along the gullies, then deep leads and shafts through Ballarat East, Golden Point and out to Sebastopol, most of them capped, filled or forgotten long before anyone thought to keep a proper register. It is not something you notice day to day. It becomes relevant when you put a container on a block, because a container does not spread its weight the way a shed does. Nearly all of the load goes through four corner castings, and four point loads will find soft or hollow ground far more efficiently than anything else you own.

If your block has any history of subsidence, a soft spot that keeps reappearing in the lawn, or a neighbour who has had a hole open up, say so when you enquire. It is not a reason not to have a container. It is a reason to put it on the firmest part of the block rather than the most convenient one, to spread the corner loads onto larger pads, and in a few cases to pick a 20ft instead of a 40ft so the loads sit inside a footprint we are confident about. That is a five-minute conversation before delivery and an expensive one afterwards.

Worth knowing

Frost, fog and what happens inside a sealed steel box

Ballarat winters are genuinely cold and genuinely damp, and that combination does something to a closed container that people are not expecting. Warm moist air gets shut in, the steel roof drops below dew point overnight, and moisture condenses on the inside of the roof and runs down the walls. People open the doors in July, see water inside, and assume the container leaks. Usually it does not. Cargo-worthy and new units are checked wind and watertight before they go out. What you are looking at is the air that was already in there.

The fixes are cheap and they work. Get the container up off the ground on hardwood sleepers or concrete pads under the corner castings so air moves underneath and the floor is not wicking damp out of the soil. Keep the vents clear rather than blocking them up. If you are storing anything that hates moisture — tools, upholstery, paper, anything with a motor — add extra vents or a passive dehumidifier and leave a gap between the stack and the walls. And take the ten minutes to check the container is level in both directions when it lands, because a unit that settles nose-down over a frosty winter turns easy doors into a two-person job by spring.

Common questions

Buying a container in Ballarat

How long does a container take to get to Ballarat?

Usually five to nine business days from a locked-in order. Containers for Ballarat come out of the Melbourne terminals and up the Western Highway, so the run itself is short. What moves the date is availability of the exact size and grade you want in Melbourne, whether a crane truck is needed, and weather — in a wet July we will often suggest waiting two fine days rather than putting a loaded truck across a soft nature strip. Give us your date at the enquiry and we will tell you honestly whether it is achievable.

My block is over old mine workings. Is that a problem?

It is a siting question, not a deal-breaker. A container carries almost all its weight through four corner castings, so it puts four concentrated point loads into the ground rather than spreading them the way a shed does. Old shafts and filled workings under Ballarat East, Golden Point and Sebastopol are not always mapped. If you know of subsidence on the block, or a soft patch that keeps coming back, tell us. We will put it on the firmest ground available, spread the corners onto larger pads, and in some cases suggest a shorter unit so the whole footprint sits where we are confident.

Why is there water inside my container in a Ballarat winter?

Nearly always condensation rather than a leak. Cold nights, damp air and a steel roof are a reliable recipe for it: moist air trapped inside hits a roof below dew point and gives up its water. Sitting the container on sleepers or pads so air moves underneath, keeping the factory vents clear and adding a couple more, and leaving an air gap between your stacked gear and the walls will deal with most of it. If you are storing anything sensitive over winter, a passive dehumidifier is worth the small cost.

Can you get a truck into a Ballarat East or Golden Point street?

Most of the time, with the right truck. The constraints in the old streets are consistent — narrow bluestone-kerbed carriageways, cars parked both sides, mature street trees with low canopies, short steep driveways and side gates built for a wheelbarrow. A tilt-tray needs a straight run and clear overhead room; where that is not there, a crane truck lifts the container over the fence instead. Send us a photo up the street and one of the kerb crossing and we will tell you which one we need before we book anything.
