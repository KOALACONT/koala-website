Shipping containers Rockhampton

The thing that catches people out here is not supply and it is not the highway. It is black soil. Heavy cracking clay opens up wide enough in October to lose a tape measure down, closes over in the wet, and will quietly take one corner of a container with it if nobody put anything underneath.

Access notes for this district

Black soil is the local specialist subject and every long-term container in this district lives or dies by how it was dealt with. Cracking clay on the Fitzroy country shrinks hard through the dry and swells through the wet, and it does not do either uniformly. A truck crossing it in October has a firm, cracked, roadlike surface. The same paddock a fortnight into a wet is a surface that will not hold a wheel, and a loaded rigid that leaves the formed track on black soil after rain is not getting itself out. Around town, the ground splits along the same lines as the flood history. Depot Hill, Port Curtis, Allenstown and the low ground near the river are alluvial and flat. The Range, Frenchville, Norman Gardens and Kawana sit higher, on better-draining country with more slope and more rock, and the driveways up there are the ones that need looking at before a tilt-tray is committed. The city grid itself is generous, laid out wide when Rockhampton was a river port, which means a truck can position properly in most streets. Out west and out to the coast, the pattern changes again. The properties toward Gracemere, Stanwell, Bajool and Duaringa are grid-and-gate rural: private grids nobody can rate, cattle on the road, long formed-earth drives, and turning space at the shed. The Capricorn Coast run to Yeppoon and Emu Park swaps that for sand and salt. And then there is heat, which is a genuine access issue here rather than a comfort one — a black steel box standing in a Rockhampton yard through a summer afternoon reaches temperatures that decide what you can sensibly keep in it and whether the unit needs venting. That is a question worth answering at the quote, not after the first February.

On the ground

Black soil will move your container if you let it

This is the one thing worth reading twice if the container is going onto clay. A shipping container carries its whole weight, plus everything you put in it, through the four castings at its corners. It is designed that way and it is fine, provided those four points stay at the same height relative to each other. Cracking clay makes no such promise. It heaves through the wet and shrinks through the dry, unevenly, and over two or three seasons it will lift one corner and drop another. Once that happens the frame is racked, and a racked container is one whose doors stop lining up. First one catches. Then it needs a bar. Then it needs a lift and a reset, which costs a great deal more than doing it right at the start.

What works out here is spreading the load onto something that will not follow the soil. Concrete pads, heavy hardwood bearers, or a pair of sleepers under each end, bedded down into firm ground and checked level across both diagonals before the truck leaves. Have the doors facing uphill wherever there is a fall. Leave a gap beneath so water drains away and air circulates instead of sitting up against the floor. And if the container is going on genuinely reactive country and it is staying for the long haul, it is worth putting it on a small prepared pad of compacted road base — an hour with a bobcat before delivery is cheaper than any of the alternatives afterwards.

Worth knowing

Heat, ventilation and what you keep inside

Rockhampton summers do things to a sealed steel box that people from further south do not always anticipate. A closed container standing in the sun gets hot, and then it stays hot well into the evening because the mass takes a long time to give it back. That is irrelevant if you are storing steel, fencing gear, panels or tools. It is very relevant if you are storing anything with a temperature range on the label — chemicals, adhesives, paint, batteries, electronics, sealed food, timber you care about, or anything with a rubber or plastic component that will go off early. Humidity plays into it as well, because a box that heats hard during the day and cools overnight will move moisture around inside itself, and that condensation has to land somewhere. On a sealed unit with no ventilation, it lands on the ceiling and drips.

There are sensible answers and they are worth deciding on before delivery rather than after. Vents fitted high and low get air moving through and take the top off the peak. A whirlybird does more. Insulation and lining is a bigger job but turns the unit into something you can work in rather than just store in. Even orientation helps — a container placed with the long side facing the afternoon sun rather than the door end has a different day to one that catches it square. Say what is going in it and how long for, and we will point at whichever of those actually earns its money rather than reading you the catalogue. For most people out here the honest answer is a pair of vents and some thought about which way the doors face, and that is where it ends.

Common questions

Buying a container in Rockhampton

Do you keep containers in Rockhampton or do they come from down south?

We hold stock at a depot in Rockhampton, so a lot of local orders go out on a unit already in town. Not every size and grade is standing there in any given week — where we do not have what you want locally it comes up from the Cornubia yard and adds a few days, and you will be told which of those it is at the quote. Come and look at one by all means, but book it in first — there is nobody on the gate to let you through unannounced.

What should a container sit on in black soil country?

Not the soil. Cracking clay swells through the wet and shrinks through the dry, unevenly, and over a couple of seasons it will lift one corner casting and drop another until the frame racks and the doors stop closing. Spread the load onto something that does not follow the ground: concrete pads, heavy hardwood bearers or sleepers under each end, bedded into firm ground and levelled across both diagonals. On genuinely reactive country that is keeping a container long-term, a small prepared pad of compacted road base is worth the hour it takes.

Will a container be too hot to store things in through a Rockhampton summer?

For fencing gear, steel, panels and tools, no — it makes no practical difference. For anything with a temperature limit on the label, or anything with rubber, plastic or adhesive in it, yes, a closed steel box standing in the sun here will get hotter than you expect and stay hot into the evening. Vents fitted high and low, a whirlybird, or lining and insulating for a working space are all options. Tell us what is going inside at the enquiry and we will suggest what genuinely helps rather than the full catalogue.

Is a container a good store on low ground near the Fitzroy?

As a lock-up, yes. As protection from the river, no, and nobody should sell it to you that way. Water that reaches the door sill works past the seals, and an empty box holds enough trapped air to lift and move. On Depot Hill, Port Curtis or anywhere on the low country, take the highest ground you can use, stay off the route water travels across the block, and stand the unit on blocks or a built pad so the floor sits clear. If it is staying down there permanently, talk to us about anchoring it.
