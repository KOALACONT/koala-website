Mount Gambier, SA

Shipping containers Mount Gambier

The whole district sits on limestone and you find out exactly how close it is the first time you try to dig a post hole. For a container that is mostly good news, because rock near the surface drains, and you are not fighting the boggy reactive clay that ruins deliveries elsewhere. The catch is what limestone does over time. Mount Gambier has caves, voids and sinkholes beneath it — some mapped, some discovered by accident — and that is worth five minutes of conversation before we set four concentrated corner loads down on a block nobody has ever questioned.

First, the things that are specific to here.

How this district uses them

Around Mount Gambier we deliver containers for chainsaw, chain and workshop storage for the harvest and haulage contractors working the plantations through Tarpeena, Kalangadoo and Nangwarry, dry gear and feed storage on the dairy and potato country round Compton, Yahl and Glencoe, barrel, netting and vineyard hardware up in the Coonawarra and Penola blocks, cray pot and boat gear at Port MacDonnell and Carpenter Rocks, secure site boxes for builders in Suttontown and Worrolong, and lock-up storage for the mills and engineering shops on the Jubilee Highway industrial land.

What the delivery actually involves

Ground here behaves better than almost anywhere else we deliver, and it is worth saying so — free-draining limestone rubble carries a truck through winters that would stop a job dead in the Goulburn Valley or the north-west of Tasmania. The complications are elsewhere. First, you cannot dig. On a great many blocks around Mount Gambier the rock is within a spade's depth, which means levelling a pad by cutting into the high side is not on the table. You level up to the high corner with hardwood sleepers or concrete pads instead, which works perfectly well but has to be planned rather than improvised on the day. Second, voids. Sinkholes and cave systems run under parts of the district, and a container is a point-load proposition rather than a spread one. If there is a known cave, a sinkhole, a depression in the paddock that keeps deepening or anything on the block that has been fenced off by a previous owner, tell us and we will keep the corner loads well clear. Third, the plantation country. Access into pine blocks around Tarpeena, Nangwarry, Mount Burr and Glencoe is generally good because it is built for log trucks, but the turning space is the landing, gates are often locked and the tracks are one-way in practice. We need a contact who can open the gate and a clear description of which landing, because there are no street numbers in a plantation. Fourth, rainfall. This is the wet corner of South Australia and winter here is genuinely wet and cold. It rarely stops a delivery on limestone ground, but on the dairy flats around Millicent and the drainage country south of town the paddocks do get soft, and the drain crossings on those properties are narrow and worth a photo before we commit a truck to one.

Mount Gambier

Limestone: the best and the worst of the ground here

Two things follow from sitting on limestone. The good one is drainage. Water goes down through this country rather than sitting on it, so the mud problems that dominate delivery planning in heavy-soil districts largely do not exist here, and a container placed on limestone rubble tends to stay exactly where you put it rather than sinking a corner over the first wet winter. That is a real advantage and it means the ground preparation conversation is shorter than usual.

The awkward one is that rock near the surface means you cannot cut a level pad. If your site has any fall, and most do, the answer is packing up to the low corners with hardwood sleepers or concrete pads until the container is level in both directions. Get that right and the doors work for the life of the unit; get it wrong and the frame racks, the doors bind and you have a two-person job every time you want something out of it. Tell us at the quote if the site slopes and we will bring the timber rather than hunting for something on your block to shim it with.

On the ground

Sinkholes, caves and where the corners go

This is not scaremongering and it is not a reason to avoid a container. It is simply that a container is unusual in how it loads the ground. Nearly all of its weight travels through four corner castings, so instead of the broad even bearing a shed gives you, you are applying four concentrated point loads. In limestone country with known voids that is worth thinking about for two minutes before delivery rather than never.

So when we quote a Mount Gambier job we ask whether there is anything known on the property — a mapped cave, a sinkhole, a fenced-off depression, a hollow that gets deeper each year, a spot the previous owner told you to stay off. If there is, we simply keep well clear and site the container on solid ground. Where the block is completely unremarkable, which is most of them, we still put the corners onto sleepers or pads to spread the load rather than sitting the castings straight on the surface. It is good practice everywhere and it is cheap insurance here.

Worth knowing

Pine country: landings, locked gates and log trucks

Delivering into plantation blocks is one of the easier jobs in the district in some ways and one of the fiddliest in others. The roads are built for loaded log trucks so surface and grade are rarely a problem. What causes the trouble is navigation and access control. Plantation compartments do not have street numbers, tracks look identical, gates are locked, and there are seasons and conditions when the block is closed to traffic entirely. A container turning up at a locked gate with nobody to open it is a wasted day.

So for a plantation delivery we want a named contact and a phone number, the compartment or landing identified in a way the driver can actually follow, confirmation the gate will be open, and a note of any restrictions on the day. Once that is sorted, the landing itself is normally an excellent place to put a container — hardstand, room to turn, and enough clearance for either a tilt-tray or a crane truck. Where the container is going somewhere off the landing, a crane truck reaching in from the hardstand is the reliable option rather than trying to drive a rigid onto soft forest floor.

Common questions

Buying a container in Mount Gambier

Are sinkholes actually a risk when siting a container here?

Worth asking about, not worth panicking about. A container puts nearly all its weight through four corner castings, so it applies four point loads rather than the spread load a shed gives you — which is why known voids matter more than they would for a shed. If there is a mapped cave, a sinkhole, a fenced-off depression or a hollow that deepens each year on the property, tell us and we will site well clear of it. On ordinary blocks we still spread the corner loads onto sleepers or concrete pads, which is good practice anywhere and cheap insurance on limestone.

I hit rock at a spade's depth. How do you level a container on that?

You do not cut, you pack. On most Mount Gambier blocks digging out the high side is not realistic, so we bring hardwood sleepers or concrete pads and build the low corners up until the unit sits level in both directions. That is what actually matters — a container carries its load through the four corners, and once those stop being at the same height the frame racks and the doors start binding. Tell us at the quote that the site is rocky and sloping and the timber comes on the truck with the container.

Can you deliver into a pine plantation block?

Yes, and the roads are usually the easy part since they are built for log trucks. The hard part is access control. We need a named contact and a mobile number, the landing or compartment identified in a way a driver can follow without street numbers, confirmation the gate will be unlocked, and any restrictions applying on the day. Landings themselves are generally ideal — hardstand and room to turn. If the container is going off the landing, a crane truck reaching from firm ground beats driving a rigid onto forest floor.

Does the container come from Adelaide or Melbourne?

Whichever has what you actually want. Mount Gambier is close to the midpoint between the two, and rather than force your order to come from one side we look at where the size and grade you have asked for is available and send it from there. That is why the window is six to eleven business days rather than a fixed number — it depends on the terminal and the run. Give us your date at the enquiry and we will tell you which way it is coming and whether the date holds.
