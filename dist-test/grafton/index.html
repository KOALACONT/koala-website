The jobs they end up doing here

Around Grafton we deliver containers for cane and machinery gear on the flats running down the Clarence toward Ulmarra and Harwood, beef, fencing and fodder storage on the grazing country out through Coutts Crossing and Copmanhurst, timber, tools and mill equipment around the industrial area at Koolkhan and South Grafton, nets, pots and marine gear for the fishing side of the valley at Maclean, Yamba and Iluka, netting, bins and irrigation fittings on the blueberry and small-crop blocks spreading north, site stores for builders working the estates at Junction Hill and Clarenza, and dry storage on the river blocks where a shed is not an option.

Getting a truck in

Having stock in town changes what is possible with a booking, and it changes what happens when something goes wrong. Next business day is genuinely on the table for a standard 20ft going to an address with clear access, we can be flexible when weather pushes a date, and a container that turns out to be the wrong size for the spot is a short run back rather than a full day of cartage. After that, Grafton is a river town and it behaves like one. The valley floor either side of the Clarence is deep alluvial silt and sand ridges, and the two do not behave the same way. The silt flats are excellent soil that dries into something that looks and feels like hardstand, then goes soft a long way down after a wet week, so a corner casting will sink while you are standing there watching it. The sand ridges through parts of South Grafton and Junction Hill drain fast and carry poorly, which is a different route to the same result. Both want the container on pads rather than straight onto turf. The town itself divides at the river. Central Grafton is a grid of wide streets with generous frontages, which is good delivering country, but it is also the jacaranda town and there is a very large amount of mature canopy sitting at four metres over the road and over the driveways. A tilt-tray raises its bed before the container comes off and it needs clear air above it, so a street that a truck drives down comfortably is not automatically a street it can unload in. South Grafton and the older river-side pockets are tighter, with short pitched driveways and low service lines. Out of town the valley opens up and the constraints become rural: long crowned gravel driveways with table drains, causeways on the tributaries that come up fast after rain in the ranges to the west even when it is dry over you, cattle grids nobody has ever rated, and gates that measure narrower at the swing than they do at the posts.

Local stock, and what that is actually worth to you

Most container sellers quoting a Clarence Valley job are working from a depot several hours away, which means the truck runs the highway both directions and that trip is in your number whether it is itemised or not. We hold stock at a depot here in Grafton. Practically, that shows up in three ways. It shows up in speed, because next business day is a real option rather than a marketing line. It shows up in flexibility, because if rain has turned your approach to grease we can move the date by two days without it costing anybody a full day of cartage. And it shows up when something is not right, because a swap is a short run rather than a negotiation.

It also means you can look at the actual unit rather than a photograph of something similar. On the ground

A river town, and where the container should sit

Grafton knows the Clarence well and there is a levee here for a reason, so let us be straight about what a container does and does not do. It is checked for weather from above. That is rain on a roof. It is not a barrier against water rising against the doors, because door seals are designed to shed rain running down a face, not to hold back a head of water pushing on them. Water that reaches the sill gets past the seals and gets inside. And an empty container is a large sealed steel box that will float and travel once the water is deep enough, which is worth thinking about on a block that has seen it before.

So the decision that matters is siting. Put it on the highest usable part of the block rather than the most convenient one, keep it clear of the overland flow path across the property, and if it is a long-term store on low ground talk to us about setting it up on pads or a raised base so the floor sits higher and the underside stays dry and ventilated. On the silt down here, spreading the load over a decent footprint matters as much as the height does, because that soil will let a concentrated point load down further than you would expect.

Worth knowing

What the valley actually uses them for

The Clarence is a working valley and the containers we put out here work. Cane and machinery gear on the river flats. Beef, fencing and fodder on the grazing country west and south. Timber and mill equipment through Koolkhan and South Grafton. Nets, pots, ropes and outboard spares down at the mouth around Maclean, Yamba and Iluka, where salt air makes a lockable steel box the sensible answer for gear that would otherwise live under a tarp. And an increasing amount of horticulture, with blueberry and small-crop blocks needing netting, bins, fittings and chemicals stored securely and out of the weather.

That mix is the reason we hold a spread of sizes here rather than a single popular one. A 10ft suits a small holding, a workshop corner or a club that needs gear locked up and nothing more. A 20ft is the workhorse and handles most farm and trade requirements. A 40ft gives machinery-length storage where the approach is good, and out on the flats the approach usually is. Where a driveway is tight but the volume is genuinely needed, two 20ft with a gap between them and a roof across the middle is a very common setup in this valley and it gives you a covered working bay for nothing much.

Buying a container in Grafton

Can I look at a container in Grafton before I buy it?

Yes, by arrangement. We hold stock at a depot in Grafton, but it is not a walk-in retail yard, so ring on 1300 467 776 first and we will set a time when someone can meet you and the unit you are interested in is accessible. How quickly can you deliver a container in the Clarence Valley?

Faster than most, because the stock is already here rather than several hours up or down the highway. Next business day happens regularly on a standard 20ft going to an address with clear access, and inside two days is the normal expectation. What stretches it is a crane booking, a unit that needs a lockbox or vents fitted before it goes, or wet weather on soft ground where waiting beats bogging a truck on your place. Tell us your date at the enquiry and we will build the run around it.

Do the jacarandas actually get in the way of a delivery in Grafton?

More often than you would think, and it catches out people who assume a wide street means an easy job. A tilt-tray raises its bed a long way before the container slides off, so it needs clear air above, not just a lane to drive down. Mature canopy hanging at around four metres over a street or a driveway is a genuine constraint, and central Grafton has a lot of it. If your frontage is under a big tree, tell us or send a photo looking up, and we will bring a crane truck or find another position rather than discovering it on the day.

Is a container a good store on a Clarence River flat?

For weather, vermin and security, yes, and it is one of the most common things we do out here. For rising water, no. A container is checked for rain from above, not for water pushing against the doors, and floodwater that reaches the sill will get past the seals. An empty one will also float once the water gets deep enough. Site it on the highest usable ground, keep it out of the overland flow path, and set it on pads or a raised base so the floor sits clear and the underside can breathe.
