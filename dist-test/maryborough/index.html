Maryborough, QLD

Shipping containers Maryborough

Maryborough is the easiest town on this stretch of coast to get a truck around, and it is not close. The surveyors laid out a proper grid with generous carriageways when the port was the point of the place, and a century and a half on, that grid still swallows a rigid truck without complaint. What it does not solve is the two things that are actually particular to Maryborough: houses built high off the ground on stumps with the good yard underneath them, and a river that has come up over Tinana and Granville often enough for everyone to have an opinion about levels.

The jobs they end up doing here

Getting a truck in

Start with the good news, because Maryborough deserves it. The old town grid — Kent, Adelaide, Bazaar, Ellena, Alice and the streets around them — has wide carriageways, generous corner radii and long straight runs, which means a tilt-tray with a 20ft on it can position properly instead of doing a three-point turn against a kerb. Very few regional towns give you that. The catch is what happens once the truck stops. Maryborough housing is high-set timber on stumps, and on a lot of blocks the only space worth putting a container is either right underneath the house, which it will not fit under, or out the back past a side gate the builder sized for a wheelbarrow in 1912. Add a mature mango, a poinciana or a camphor laurel with limbs down at three and a half metres and a lifted tilt bed has nowhere to go. On those blocks a crane truck standing in the street and lifting over the house is not exotic, it is just the way it is done. The other half of the district is the flats. Tinana, Granville, Baddow, Aldershot and the low country along the Mary and Tinana Creek sit on alluvial silt and heavy river soil. That ground carries a truck fine through the dry months and turns to something else entirely after a wet week, and it holds water long after the surface has crusted over. Cane country out toward Owanyilla and Yengarie brings farm tracks, headland turns that are tighter than they look from the road, and irrigation mains under crossings that are not built for twenty tonne stopped on top of them. If your address is on the flats, tell us what the last stretch is and when it last rained, and if it is in the old town, tell us about the side gate and the trees rather than the street. The street is almost never the problem here.

High-set houses and the side gate problem

The single most common Maryborough conversation is about a block where the street is wide, the driveway is fine and the container still cannot get to where you want it. Timber houses on stumps take up the middle of the lot, the side setback is narrow, and whatever gate exists was cut for a person and a mower. A tilt-tray needs to reverse into a straight run and slide the unit off along the ground, and it needs that run to be clear the whole way, not clear for the first four metres. Once there is a house corner, a tank stand or a stump line in the way, that is the end of it. It is worth measuring the narrowest point of the side access before you order rather than eyeing it off, because the tilt bed is wider than the container and gateposts on these old blocks have usually been clipped once already.

So we lift instead. A crane truck sets up on the road, picks the container up and puts it down in the back yard over the house or the fence. Everything that decides whether it works can be measured: the horizontal distance from wherever the truck can legally stand, the height of the ridgeline or the trees it must clear, anything strung overhead at the front of the property, and whether the carriageway leaves room for outriggers. Maryborough streets generally do give that room, which is precisely why crane work here is more straightforward than it is in most towns of this size. Three photographs answer nearly all of it: one from the footpath looking at the house, one taken from where you want the container standing looking back toward the road, and one looking straight up at whatever crosses your frontage.

On the ground

The flats, the river and honest expectations

Tinana, Granville, Baddow and the low ground along the river have been under water more than once in living memory, and anyone buying a container to store things on that country should hear the same thing we tell everyone. A container is an excellent lock-up and it keeps rain out from above. It is not a flood barrier. Once the level touches the door sill it finds its way past the seals, and an empty steel box with air trapped in it lifts and travels once the water gets deep enough. That is not a reason to avoid one. It is a reason to put it in the right place, and to think about what sits on the floor of it versus what sits on a shelf at chest height, because that single decision is what most people wish they had made differently afterwards.

The right place is high. Take the best ground you have, keep off the route water travels when it crosses the block, and where there is no high ground to be had, stand the unit on blocks or a built pad so the floor sits above the surface with air moving under it. On the flats there is a second reason to do that anyway, which is that heavy river soil holds moisture for weeks and a floor bearing on damp ground for years does not thank you for it. If the container is going to live down there long-term, talk to us about anchoring as well, so it stays put rather than travelling if it ever gets buoyant. A unit that moves in a flood stops being your problem alone the moment it ends up against somebody else's fence.

Worth knowing

Character streets and what the neighbours see

Maryborough takes its heritage seriously and large parts of the old town sit inside character and heritage precincts. That does not usually stop a container going onto a block, but it does change where it can sensibly sit and how visible it should be, and there are situations — anything permanent, anything altering a listed building, anything prominent from a street with a heritage character — where the council position is worth checking before you order rather than after. We are not going to guess at the rule for your particular lot, because it varies, it changes, and it is yours to confirm rather than ours to promise. What we can tell you is that the question comes up often enough here to be worth a phone call to council at the planning stage, and that the people on the counter deal with it regularly and are not difficult about it.

The practical guidance is the same as it is anywhere, just applied a bit more carefully here. Set back from the road, softened by a fence or a bit of planting, and not the first thing anybody notices walking past will keep the neighbours and the council equally relaxed. Sitting on the front lawn of a Queenslander in one of the character streets will not. And where you intend to clad it, paint it something that vanishes into the yard, or hang a lean-to off one side, decide that before delivery rather than after, because the finish is far easier to apply with clear space on all four sides. Moving a container twenty metres once the truck has gone is a second delivery, not a tweak, and it is charged like one.

Common questions

Buying a container in Maryborough

My house at Maryborough is high-set with a narrow side gate. Can a container get to the back yard?

Not on a tilt-tray, most likely, but yes with a crane truck. It stands in the street, picks the container up over the house or the side fence and sets it down where you want it. Maryborough streets are wide enough to get outriggers out properly, which makes this easier here than in most towns of the size. Tell us how far it is from the road to the spot, how high the roof or the trees in between are, and whether anything is strung overhead at the front. Two photos, one from the footpath and one from the back yard, will usually settle it.

How long does a container take to reach Maryborough?

The Gympie depot and the short run north up the Bruce are what set it. It is quicker where a standard 20ft is already standing at our Gympie depot and the approach at your end is clear, and it stretches where a crane has to be booked, where you have asked for a specific grade or configuration that has to come from Cornubia, or where wet weather has put a river-flat address out of action for a couple of days. If there is a fixed date on your end, tell us when you enquire.

Can I put a container on a block at Tinana or Granville that has flooded before?

You can, and plenty do, but choose the spot for elevation rather than for how close it is to the house. A container will not hold water out — once the level touches the door sill it gets past the seals, and an empty one lifts and travels once it is deep enough. Take the best ground on the block, keep off the route water takes when it crosses, and where the whole lot is low, stand it on blocks or a built pad so the floor sits clear. For anything living down there permanently, anchoring is worth talking through.

Do I need approval for a container in a Maryborough heritage or character street?

That turns on how long it is staying, how obvious it is from the footpath, and whether your property or the precinct around it carries a listing — and we are not going to guess at your particular lot. Ring council at the planning stage rather than after the truck has been. As a rule of thumb, short-term and screened almost never causes trouble, while permanent, prominent or modified is where somebody starts asking questions. Answering it early is much cheaper than paying for a second delivery to shift the thing.
