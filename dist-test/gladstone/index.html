Shipping containers Gladstone

Gladstone runs on projects, and projects run on dates. A shutdown has a start and a finish written on a wall somewhere, and everything feeding it either lands inside that window or is a problem. That is the main thing we plan around here — not the drive, which is straightforward, but the gate. The other half of the district is domestic, and Gladstone housing is built across some genuinely steep ground with cut-and-fill blocks and retaining walls that decide where a container can go long before you do.

What the site has to allow for

There are two Gladstones for a delivery driver and they have almost nothing in common. The industrial side is technically easy and administratively particular. Hardstand, wide gates, room to swing, a forklift or a crane usually already on site — but also inductions, gate passes, escorts, permits to work, high-visibility and boot requirements, and nominated delivery windows that are enforced rather than suggested. A truck without paperwork does not get through the boom, and that is nobody's fault but it is everybody's delay. Sort the site requirements out with your contractor or the site coordinator first, tell us what the driver needs to carry and what time the window is, and the job runs like clockwork. The domestic side is where the driving gets interesting. Gladstone is built over hills and the residential suburbs — West Gladstone, Kin Kora, Sun Valley, Barney Point, Telina, Clinton — are full of blocks that have been cut into or filled out from a slope, with retaining walls, short steep driveways and level changes between the street and the yard. A driveway that turns and rises at the same time is the one that gets a tilt-tray. The bed grounds out on the transition, or the truck cannot straighten before it runs out of driveway. On plenty of those a crane lift from the street is the only sensible method, and the constraint becomes reach across a retaining wall and the height of anything in between. Out at Tannum Sands, Boyne Island and the coastal edge you swap hills for sand and salt air. Further inland toward Calliope, Mount Larcom and the grazing country it is rural: unsealed approaches, grids of unknown rating, creek crossings that come up fast in the wet, and turning space at the shed that needs to be actual rather than assumed.

Getting a truck through the gate

Industrial delivery in Gladstone is not hard, it is procedural, and the failures are almost always paperwork rather than access. Every major site here runs its own induction, its own permit system and its own delivery scheduling, and the requirements are not identical between them. The transport operator generally needs a nominated arrival window, a contact who will actually meet the truck, evidence of insurance, and whatever the site demands of a driver in terms of induction and personal protective equipment. Some sites want a vehicle inspection at the gate. Some want an escort from the boom to the laydown area. None of it is unreasonable and none of it is negotiable at seven in the morning with a loaded truck idling at the entry, which is where these things get discovered when nobody has asked in advance.

What we ask for at the booking is simple: the site, the gate you want the truck to use, the window you have been given, the name and number of the person meeting it, and any induction or documentation the driver has to have. Give us that and the container lands in the right place at the right hour. Leave it vague and the truck sits at a boom gate burning your window and ours. If your delivery is tied to a shutdown that has a hard start, say so early and we will build backwards from that date rather than forwards from the order, because a container that lands the day after the crews arrive has already failed at the only thing it was there to do. The same goes for demobilisation — tell us the finish date at the start and the pick-up gets scheduled with everything else rather than becoming a phone call you make in a hurry.

Hire that matches a campaign

Shutdown and project work is exactly the situation hire was invented for. You need secure, weatherproof storage on a laydown for six or ten or fourteen weeks, you need it on site before the crews arrive, and you need it gone once demobilisation is finished. Owning the asset for that is the wrong shape — you carry it on the books, you store it between campaigns, and you organise the trucking at both ends. Hire puts a date on it, gets it off site when the job is done, and lets you scale the number up or down as the scope changes, which on a campaign it invariably does. It also keeps the cost sitting against the job rather than against the balance sheet, which is usually where a project manager wants it.

Buying makes better sense where the container becomes permanent infrastructure — a consumables store on a yard you lease at Clinton, a spares lock-up you will still have in five years, a store on a grazing property out at Calliope. It also makes sense where you want to modify it, because a hire unit comes back the way it went out. If it is going to live outdoors on an industrial site holding anything you need to stay dry, ask for cargo-worthy, which is checked wind and watertight before it leaves the yard. Tell us the duration and what is going inside and we will tell you which way round it should be, including the case where the answer is one bought unit for the permanent store and two hired ones over the top of a campaign. That combination is common here and it is usually the cheapest way through.

Worth knowing

Cut, fill and where it can actually land

Gladstone housing is built across slopes, and a great many blocks have been made level by cutting into the hill on one side and filling out on the other. That has two consequences worth thinking about before you order. The first is that the flat part of your yard may be fill, and fill that has not been through many wet seasons behaves quite differently from the cut side. The second is that retaining walls are structural and they have a design load that did not contemplate a container sitting against them or a truck standing on the fill behind them. Setting a six-metre steel box hard up against the top of a retaining wall is a conversation with an engineer, not a delivery decision.

In practice we look for the firmest, most level, least loaded part of the block, which is often not the spot the customer had in mind. Getting the unit onto the cut side or onto an existing slab or driveway apron is worth losing a few metres of convenience for. Where the fall across the block is significant, we build the low side up with pads or bearers instead of digging the high side out, because cutting into a slope only carves a trench for water to sit in alongside the unit. Get the door end onto the high side of the fall while you are at it, so the doors swing with gravity rather than against it. That distinction gets missed constantly and it is the difference between a store you open with one hand for twenty years and one that needs a shoulder by the second winter.

Common questions

Buying a container in Gladstone

Can you deliver a container onto a Gladstone industrial site with induction requirements?

Yes, and it is routine, but the paperwork has to be sorted before the day. Tell us at the booking which site and which gate, the delivery window you have been given, who is meeting the truck, and exactly what the driver needs to hold in the way of induction, insurance evidence and protective equipment. Some sites require an escort from the boom to the laydown. Give us all of that up front and the job runs to time. Leave it until the morning and the truck sits at the gate.

Can I hire containers just for the length of a shutdown?

That is one of the main reasons hire exists here. You set a start and a finish, we get the units on the laydown before the crews arrive and off site once demobilisation is done, and you are not carrying an asset or organising storage between campaigns. If the scope changes mid-campaign, the number of units can change with it. Tell us the dates, the site access requirements and roughly what is going inside, and we will quote the whole thing including getting them there and away again.

My block at Kin Kora is on a slope with a retaining wall. Where can the container go?

Usually somewhere, but probably not where you first pictured. Retaining walls have a design load and a container plus contents sitting on the fill behind one is not something to decide on the day — that is an engineering question. We look for the firmest and most level part of the block, which is often the cut side, an existing slab or the driveway apron. Where there is a fall, we build the low side up with pads or bearers rather than cutting the high side away, which only makes a channel for water to pond against the unit.

How long does delivery to Gladstone take?

Site deliveries are scheduled around your induction and gate access rather than around our run, so a firm window at your end tightens ours. It stretches where a crane truck is needed, where a particular grade or size has to come north, or where wet weather has made a rural approach out toward Calliope or Ubobo unsafe for a loaded truck. If the date is tied to a shutdown start, tell us early and we will plan backwards from it.
