The case for it

Where a 10ft earns its place

  • Slips past the side of an ordinary suburban home where a twenty has no chance. On a standard side passage that fact alone usually settles the argument.
  • Comes off an ordinary tilt-tray and wants about half the approach a twenty needs, so a lot of sites that would otherwise fail become workable.
  • Usually works out dearer per cubic metre than a twenty, because supply is thin and the build is not simply a cut-down box. Where there is room for the longer box, ask for both figures before deciding, and you will hear which one suits from us rather than an easy yes.

What actually goes in one

A ride-on, a bench and shelving up the walls — roughly what a single lock-up garage swallows. A two-bedroom unit's worth of furniture will go in if it is packed hard and nothing in it is fragile.

The bit people wish they had known

Ten-footers are the tightest supply of any size in the country. Used ones can turn over inside a week, so a figure somebody gave you last month may not hold today. Ring and ask what is physically standing at Cornubia, or at whichever depot is nearest you.

Grade matters more than anything else in a container quote. Grade is what decides whether the box keeps water out. Anything sold as cargo-worthy is inspected wind and watertight before release — for storage use, which is separate from the CSC certification an export job needs — and new single-trip units, one loaded voyage with rubbers that have barely weathered, are sealed as well. As-is is the cheap end and carries no watertight claim at all: expect a previous repair, a floor section that has gone soft, or a gasket that no longer pulls up hard against the frame. We list those faults and photograph them on request, before delivery, so nothing turns up as a surprise when the truck does. If what goes inside has to stay dry, start at cargo-worthy.

Delivery and access

In detail

Choosing a 10ft

Is a 10 foot container big enough for the job?

The cubic figure flatters it. 15.9m³ sounds generous until you allow for reaching things, and the honest comparison is a single lock-up garage bay at full container height — about 2.39m of clear headroom, which is more than most garages give you. That height is where a 10ft quietly wins: shelving to the roof, a ride-on underneath, and a bench along one wall still leaves a walkway.

Pallets are the test that settles most arguments. Two Australian standard pallets sit across the width of a 10ft with very little to spare, and the door opening is 2.34m wide by 2.28m high, so anything long or awkward has to swing in through that gap rather than appear inside by magic. Measure the longest thing you own before you settle the length. The full figures, alongside the 20ft and 40ft, are on the container dimensions page.

10ft or 20ft — where the ten actually wins, and where it does not

A 10ft is not the cheap way to buy room, and we would rather say so than sell you the wrong box. Ten-footers are built short deliberately rather than cut down from a twenty, supply of them is the thinnest of any size in the country, and the result is that you almost always pay more per cubic metre than you would for a 20ft shipping container. If the ground will take the longer unit, the longer unit is usually the better buy.

What a 10ft buys you is access, and access is not a nice-to-have. It slips down the side passage of an ordinary suburban house where a twenty has no chance, it comes off a standard tilt-tray rather than needing a crane, and it wants roughly half the straight approach. On a pinched block, a short setback or an inner-city courtyard, that is the difference between having a container and not having one. It is also the sensible pick when the job is genuinely small — a lock-up for the mower, the compressor and a wall of hand tools, or a secure store on a site with no shed.

Ring with the address and a description of the entry and you will get both figures side by side, the ten and the twenty, with the delivery worked into each. Where the twenty fits, we will tell you it fits.

Siting a 10ft on a tight block

A 10ft weighs about 1,300 kg empty and puts that load through four corner castings rather than spreading it across the footprint, so what sits under those four corners matters more than the ground in between. Concrete pavers, sleepers or short concrete pads at each corner are the usual answer. Level it properly before it is loaded, because doors that shut sweetly on a level box will bind on a twisted one and no amount of adjusting the locking bars fixes a racked frame.

Leave yourself room in front of the doors. They swing through a wide arc and need roughly 2.5m of clear ground to open fully, which is the detail most often discovered after the truck has gone. Keep the unit off a fence line if you can, give the roof somewhere to drain, and if it is going on grass or soil, expect the corners to sink unevenly through a wet season unless they are bearing on something solid.

Access at the street end is a separate question, and it is the one that decides which truck comes. There is more on it, including what to photograph before you enquire, on the delivery and access page.

Can a 10ft be shifted later?

Yes, and that is one of the quieter arguments for the size. A ten is the easiest container in the range to relocate because it goes back onto an ordinary tilt-tray, so a move within a property, across town or to a new address is a straightforward job rather than a crane booking. Plenty of people buy a 10ft precisely because it is not a permanent decision.

The catch is that it has to come out the way it went in. If a fence panel came down, a tree was trimmed or a slab was still being poured on delivery day, the same clearances have to exist again on the way out. Empty it before it moves — a loaded container is not a container that can be dragged — and if it has been sitting on soil for a few years, allow time to get it lifted clear rather than winched. A container that has been modified with a roller door, windows or lining moves just as easily; modification work does not lock it in place.

Grades on a 10ft, and what each one promises

The grade decides more than the length ever will. New single-trip means one loaded voyage, rubbers that have barely weathered and a floor nothing has been dropped on. Cargo-worthy used is the volume grade and is inspected wind and watertight before it is released, which is a storage standard and separate from the CSC certification an export job needs. As-is is the cheap end and carries no watertight claim at all — expect a previous repair, a soft section of floor or a gasket that no longer pulls up hard against the frame.

Because 10ft supply is so thin, what is available in a given grade moves week to week far more than it does on a twenty. A figure somebody quoted last month may not hold today, and the useful question is not what a list says but what is physically standing at Cornubia or at the depot nearest your address. Ring and you will be told exactly that. The grade descriptions in full are on the container grades page, and if you would rather not own it at all, a 10ft can go out on container hire by the week.

Questions

The 10ft, answered

What are the dimensions of a 10ft shipping container?

Outside, 2.99m × 2.44m × 2.59m. Inside, 2.83m × 2.35m × 2.39m, which gives 15.9m³ of usable room, through a door opening of 2.34m × 2.28m, at an empty weight of approx 1,300 kg. Those are ISO figures and they hold whoever built the box, so there is no point shopping on them. What does differ from one 10ft to the next is how sound the floor is and how hard the doors pull up against the seal, and that is a grade question rather than a dimensions one.

What fits in a 10ft container?

A ride-on, a bench and shelving up the walls — roughly what a single lock-up garage swallows. A two-bedroom unit's worth of furniture will go in if it is packed hard and nothing in it is fragile.

How much does a 10ft shipping container cost?

It is priced on the grade — new single-trip, cargo-worthy used or as-is — and quoted for the exact unit with delivery to your address, in AUD ex GST. Where any individual 10ft lands comes down to its condition, to what is physically standing in the yard the week you ring, and to which yard it has to be released from. Cartage is worked out per address and quoted alongside the box rather than published, because the access at the far end shifts it as much as the distance does.

What does a 10ft container need for delivery?

A ten lands off a standard tilt-tray and wants roughly half the approach a twenty does — around 12 metres of straight run, 3 metres clear where it pinches, and 4.5 metres overhead while the tray tips. That is precisely why the size exists. Tight suburban blocks, pinched side passages and awkward courtyards in the inner city become workable at this length and stay impossible at anything longer.

Can I hire a 10ft rather than buy one?

Yes. Hire earns its keep when the container has a finish date on it — a build, a fit-out, a harvest, a rebuild after storm damage. If it is still going to be sitting there in two years, buying is nearly always the cheaper end of the deal by a wide margin. Give us the period and both numbers get run for you.

Is a 10 foot container the same as a 10ft shipping container?

Same thing. “10ft”, “10 foot” and “ten-footer” all describe a container about 2.99m long externally, and the trade uses the three interchangeably. The measurement is nominal and taken from the outside, which is why the inside comes back at 2.83m rather than a round three metres.

What is the difference between a 10ft and a 20ft shipping container?

Length, and almost nothing else. Both are 2.44m wide and 2.59m high externally, both take the same doors, the same locking bars and the same lock box, and both come in the same grades. A 20ft is roughly double the floor area, takes about ten Australian standard pallets on the floor against two across the width of a ten, and is usually cheaper per cubic metre because supply of twenties is deep and supply of tens is not. A 10ft goes onto a tilt-tray and wants about half the approach, which is the whole reason to choose one.

Can I move a 10ft shipping container after it has been delivered?

Yes. A 10ft is the easiest size in the range to relocate because it goes back onto a standard tilt-tray rather than needing a crane. Empty it first, and make sure the access that got it in still exists — if a fence came down or a tree was trimmed on delivery day, the same clearances are needed on the way out.

Are 10ft shipping containers watertight?

It depends entirely on the grade. New single-trip and cargo-worthy used units are inspected wind and watertight before they are released, for storage use. As-is units are not sold watertight and carry no such claim — they are the cheap end precisely because something on them is past its best, and we will ask what is going inside before letting anybody buy one on price alone. If the contents have to stay dry, start at cargo-worthy.

How much room do I need to open the doors on a 10ft?

About 2.5m of clear ground in front of the doors for them to swing through their full arc, plus somewhere to stand while you load. It is the measurement people forget, because the truck fits and the container fits and then the doors meet a fence. Check it before you decide where the box lands, not after.
